READING NOTE

Before comparing the numbers

Each source sees a different part of the harvest.

A satellite field estimate, a farm survey, a crop-condition score, a variety trial and a national balance sheet are all useful. They are not interchangeable measurements of one thing.

“The aim is not to decide which number wins. It is to show what each instrument observed, where the evidence aligns, where it pulls apart and what the final statistics will test.”
Editorial principle for the 2026 evidence record
Our field recordGreat Britain winter wheat inferred field by field from satellite observations — yield from imagery to 1 August, field mapping updated to 17 August.
AHDB harvest surveyWheat yields reported by participating farms from the crop harvested so far, using AHDB’s published nationalisation and moisture procedure.
AHDB crop developmentThe share of winter-wheat area assessed in five condition classes. It describes condition, not tonnes per hectare.
Recommended ListsManaged variety-trial performance relative to controls. The sites are not designed to represent commercial national yield.
USDA and DefraUnited Kingdom all-wheat production or final official area, yield and production—broader targets than Great Britain winter wheat.

02 / TIME

The season can be replayed

A forecast is more useful when its development is visible.

The crop reached its national canopy maximum in the third week of May, then matured rapidly. The weekly record lets a reader see when the estimate stabilised—and whether a revision came from mapped area, crop state or calibration.

Why the canopy checks matterHistorical association

A simpler route lands in the same range.

Season-integrated LAI and NDVI use fewer modelling assumptions than the main yield system. They do share Sentinel-2 observations, crop masks and processing, so they are corroborative rather than fully independent validations.

What happened after 1 August?Timing risk

The yield estimate cannot see later physical losses.

England’s July 2026 rainfall was only 6.5 mm—10% of normal—so the wet-harvest mechanism seen in 2023 was less likely. Post-cutoff loss is still possible; it stays a disclosed risk here, and no adjustment is applied for it — none was pre-specified and validated out of sample.

03 / METHOD

From observation to supply

One traceable chain, with observed and inferred quantities kept separate.

Satellite reflectance is observed. Crop classification, leaf area, yield and uncertainty are derived or modelled. Showing those steps makes the result easier to interrogate and harder to overstate.

Where the evidence aligns

Dry, early and highly variable.

Condition deteriorated through spring; harvested farm results span a very wide range; trial yields sit below their recent benchmark; the map concentrates losses in drier southern and eastern areas; and sampled milling grain often retained strong quality.

Where it still differs

The national yield level remains unresolved.

Our record estimates 7.70 t/ha for Great Britain winter wheat. AHDB’s early harvest estimate is 6.7 t/ha for wheat reported so far. The gap is material; its meaning depends on scope, sample, timing and moisture convention.

Moisture sensitivity

Dry grain weighs less—even when dry matter is unchanged.

equivalent yield = yield × (1 − source moisture) / (1 − target moisture)

Why this does not close the gap

Treat it as a sensitivity—not a reconciliation.

AHDB adjusts survey yields above 14.5% moisture down but does not uplift yields below 14.5%. Defra has used the same asymmetric convention since 2022. Our historical target spans that convention change unless it has been independently normalised to a fixed moisture or dry-matter basis.

A common-basis reconstruction is needed before attributing a precise share of the 1.0 t/ha difference to moisture.

The caveat we publish ourselves: the record attenuates extreme years (out-of-fold slope 0.42). If 2026 truly is survey-weak, Defra can land below 7.0 — our reading cannot rule that out. Either way this call is frozen, and will be scored.

What might move the comparison next?

Later northern harvest returns could change the farm-survey estimate; the final wheat area could move the production comparison; and official regional results will test whether the north–south ranking appears in a different measurement system.

Why are crop-condition and trial regressions not shown as national forecasts here?

They answer different questions and are based on only seven or eight historical seasons. The trial network also changes as results arrive. They remain useful technical context, but converting them into co-equal national yield numbers creates more apparent precision than the evidence supports.

How is USDA production compared here?

USDA publishes production, not yield, so a yield comparison exists only under a stated area assumption. The ledger converts the 12.00 Mt August figure using our mapped 1.558 Mha and a 93–96% winter share of UK wheat area, giving an implied 7.06–7.43 t/ha; the production chart compares the tonnage directly, where the scopes differ only by spring wheat and Northern Ireland.

The test is set now — before Defra publishes 2026.

The comparison to the official UK all-wheat result uses the bridge declared in 05 — the ~+0.35 winter-wheat premium and the delivered-moisture conversion — and both targets are also shown unbridged, never as if identical.
